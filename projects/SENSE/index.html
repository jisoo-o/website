Abstract

Existing EEG-to-speech approaches formulate the task as acoustic reconstruction, optimizing waveform fidelity while ignoring whether the generated speech preserves semantic content. We argue that EEG signals carry semantic as well as acoustic information, and that two limitations follow from the acoustic-only framing.

First, prior encoders treat the 128 electrodes as unordered feature dimensions, ignoring their spatial relationships. Second, they use the N400 corpus in a paradigm-agnostic manner, although the corpus is built around a congruency distinction designed to isolate semantic processing.

SENSE addresses both. A graph-based encoder models the electrodes over their scalp geometry, and EEG Semantic Conditioning (ESC) aligns the EEG latent with a frozen CLIP text embedding using congruent trials only. On the N400 dataset, SENSE outperforms prior methods on both acoustic and semantic metrics, with the largest gains on unseen participants.

Method

Structure-aware encoding and semantic conditioning

  • Graph-based spatial encoder. The 128 electrodes are represented as graph nodes with 3D BioSemi coordinates, connected by a fixed adjacency built from a Gaussian kernel over pairwise distances. Two residual graph-convolution layers mix information spatially. A parallel CNN branch preserves channel-specific detail that node averaging would otherwise attenuate, and a learnable scalar gate combines the two branches.
  • EEG Semantic Conditioning. The EEG latent is projected into the decoder conditioning space and aligned with a frozen CLIP text embedding under a cosine loss restricted to congruent trials, where the neural response reflects coherent semantic processing of the target word. Incongruent trials still contribute to the acoustic and phoneme objectives.
  • Conditioning bridge. During training the decoder is conditioned on one of three sources with roughly equal probability: the EEG-derived vector, the CLIP text embedding, or a learnable null embedding. This keeps the EEG vector in distribution when text conditioning is unavailable at inference.

  • ESC depends on correct semantic targets. Permuting the CLIP targets within each batch, while keeping the same loss and conditioning bridge, lowers BERT-R to 0.072, slightly below removing ESC entirely (0.076). The gain therefore comes from genuine EEG-semantic correspondence rather than auxiliary supervision alone.
  • Anatomy-derived adjacency outperforms learned graphs. Compared against GAT-learned edges, fully-connected, and random adjacency, the fixed BioSemi topology is best on every metric (WER 0.995 against 1.018, 1.095, and 1.081). Learning the graph from only 7,200 training pairs is under-constrained, while removing structure entirely is more harmful.
  • CLIP is the strongest alignment target. CLIP reaches BERT-R 0.109, ahead of text-only SBERT (0.094) and CLAP (0.092), despite CLAP being trained for audio-language alignment. A likely explanation is that N400 stimuli often involve concrete, image-able nouns, for which CLIP image-text pretraining provides more discriminative semantic structure.
  • ESC complements rather than replaces the encoder. Adding ESC to FESDE and FE-Phoneme improves both (BERT-R 0.045 and 0.070), but neither reaches SENSE.

Analysis

Channel attribution

The channel attention gate is initialized with a positive bias on auditory-related electrodes and zero elsewhere. No prior is placed on sensorimotor or centro-parietal regions. After training, the auditory-prior channels are modestly down-weighted while central electrodes (C3, C4) gain importance, so the resulting pattern emerges from training rather than design.

The prominence of C3 and C4 is consistent with prior findings that speech perception engages sensorimotor cortex during passive listening. We treat these maps as model-internal diagnostics rather than evidence of neural sources, given the known limitations of saliency-based attribution.
