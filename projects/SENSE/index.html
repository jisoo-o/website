SENSE: Semantic Neural Speech Synthesis from Brain Dynamics via Spatial Graph Encoding

EEG-to-speech has been treated as an acoustic problem. We show the brain signal carries meaning too — and that using it changes what the model can do.

Jisoo Park¹*, Seonghak Lee¹, Hyojin Park², Junseok Kwon¹†

¹Chung-Ang University, South Korea  ·  ²University of Birmingham, United Kingdom

susiehome@cau.ac.kr

* Work done during a research visit to the University of Birmingham.  † Corresponding author.

The idea

Every EEG-to-speech model so far optimizes waveform fidelity: make the audio look right and hope the words follow. They don't. You get speech-shaped sound with the wrong words in it.

We think the framing is wrong. EEG recorded while someone listens carries semantic information, not just acoustic — and the standard benchmark hands you a way to use it. The N400 corpus pairs each sentence frame with either a congruent or an incongruent final word, precisely to isolate semantic processing. Prior models train on both identically, throwing that structure away.

SENSE changes two things. It treats the 128 electrodes as a graph over their real scalp geometry instead of an unordered feature vector, and it aligns the EEG latent to a frozen CLIP text embedding using congruent trials only — the trials where the brain response actually reflects coherent understanding of the word.

Method

How it works

  • The electrodes form a graph. 128 nodes at their real BioSemi 3D coordinates, connected by a Gaussian kernel over pairwise distance. Two residual graph-conv layers mix them spatially. A parallel CNN branch recovers channel-specific detail that node-averaging would smear away, and a learnable gate blends the two.
  • Semantic conditioning, congruent trials only. The EEG latent is projected into the decoder's conditioning space and pulled toward a frozen CLIP text embedding. Restricting this to congruent trials is the trick — incongruent trials still train the acoustic and phoneme objectives, they just don't get to poison the semantic target.
  • A bridge so the decoder accepts it. During training the decoder is conditioned at random on one of three things: the EEG vector, the CLIP text vector, or a learnable null. That keeps the EEG vector in-distribution once text disappears at test time.

  • The semantic signal is real, not just regularization. Shuffle the CLIP targets within each batch — same loss, same conditioning bridge, wrong labels — and BERT-R falls to 0.072, below removing ESC entirely (0.076). The gain depends on the targets being correct.
  • Anatomy beats a learned graph. We tried GAT-learned edges, fully-connected, and random adjacency. Fixed BioSemi geometry wins on every metric (WER 0.995 vs. 1.018 / 1.095 / 1.081). With only 7,200 training pairs, learning the graph is under-constrained — but discarding structure is worse still.
  • CLIP beats CLAP, which is surprising. CLAP is trained for audio–language alignment, so it ought to suit a speech task better. It doesn't (BERT-R 0.092 vs CLIP's 0.109), and it even loses to text-only SBERT (0.094). Our guess: N400 stimuli lean on concrete, imageable nouns — chair, dog, ants — where CLIP's visual grounding gives sharper structure.
  • ESC helps the baselines too, but not enough. Bolting it onto FESDE and FE-Phoneme improves both (BERT-R 0.045 and 0.070), yet neither reaches SENSE. Encoder and conditioning are complementary, not interchangeable.

Analysis

The model finds motor cortex on its own

The channel gate starts with a small positive bias on auditory electrodes and zero everywhere else. Nothing favors sensorimotor or centro-parietal channels. After training, the auditory prior is down-weighted and C3/C4 — motor cortex — light up.

This lines up with a known result — that passive listening engages sensorimotor cortex — and supports the premise that EEG-to-speech benefits from channels outside auditory regions. We read these as model-internal diagnostics, not neural source localization. Saliency maps have well-documented limits.

Honest limitations

What this does not show

  • The words are mostly still wrong. WER near 1.0 means word-for-word reconstruction generally fails. The relative improvement is large; the absolute result is not usable.
  • One controlled corpus. Full waveform synthesis is evaluated only on N400. Naturalistic and active-speech datasets are the obvious next step.
  • Not thought decoding. This uses a public benchmark collected under prior ethical approval, with no new recruitment or recording. Listening in a lab is not a safeguard; neural data deserves on-device processing, opt-in consent, and real governance, per the UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Neurotechnology (2025).
